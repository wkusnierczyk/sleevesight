# Sleevesight privacy policy

Last updated: 26 October 2026.

This policy covers Sleevesight, the Android app (package `app.sleevesight`), and this website. Sleevesight is open source: the code that does everything described here is on [GitHub](https://github.com/wkusnierczyk/sleevesight). Near the end, it says where Sleevesight stands under the EU's data protection law, [the GDPR](#the-gdpr), and its law on artificial intelligence, [the AI Act](#the-eu-ai-act).

## In short

Sleevesight has no server, no accounts and no ads, and no telemetry, crash reporting or analytics of its own; if any is added, it will never carry your data. Nothing you play, and nothing the app sends on its own, reaches its developer: only a report does (of a problem with an insight, or a tester's report of the app's state), and only if you send it yourself, from your own mail app, which until then handles it as any message you write and may keep a draft in your mail account. To write about the song you are playing, the app sends the track's names from your phone straight to the sources you switch on and to the AI service you choose, using your own API keys. Google Play's data safety section counts that as data the app collects, since it leaves the phone. Everything else stays on your phone, apart from a diagnostics export you make yourself, a problem report you hand to your mail app, and, while you type a key on a computer, the phone's Bluetooth advertisement, which devices nearby can see: a random identifier for that session, and the phone's name only with *Share phone's name with Type on a computer* on.

## What the app reads on the phone

Sleevesight needs notification access, which is how Android lets an app read what a music player is playing. To find your music players, it looks at apps that have a media session, playing or paused, and checks whether they play music, a track with a title and an artist: from an app you have not switched on, it reads what the app's media session says it is playing only to tell whether that is music, and only until it is; after that it reads nothing more of what the app plays until Sleevesight starts afresh (not each time you open it), when it checks again. Besides that, it reads only which app the session belongs to and whether Android marks it as a call, as it does for every session. A new player is added to its settings switched off. Sleevesight does not follow a player that is off: it does not track what the player plays, and keeps or sends none of it; the player itself is kept in its list. To show those players by name, it can see which apps on the phone have a launcher icon, offer media browsing to other apps, or take media buttons, and reads the name and the icon of each player it lists; Android tells it when one of those apps is uninstalled, so it leaves the list. It keeps no list of your apps and sends none. It follows only the players you switch on (Spotify by default). From them it reads which player it is; the title, artists, album (with its year, when the player sends one, shown after it), length and cover; whether it is playing, and how far into the track; and the queue of tracks up next, with its name. It ignores the notifications themselves, and it never touches the audio. Calls from the phone's own call apps (the dialer and Android's telecom service), and any media session Android marks as a voice call, are never read: such a session, which may carry the caller's name or number, is dropped before anything of it is read, whatever the settings say. Android's mark is honoured from the moment Android reports it: whatever a calling app reported before it marked its session as a call is treated like any player's. Once a session has been seen as a call, Sleevesight ignores it while Android marks it and for a third of a second after it next reports with the mark off, and then reads it afresh, like any player's. An app you follow that also makes calls (not the phone app, which is never read) may show leftover caller details as a track for a moment after a call, and a call Sleevesight did not see (after a restart, or while notification access is off) is read like any player's once Android no longer marks it. A calling app that shows its call as an ordinary media session is not recognised as a call: like any player, it is checked for music, and followed only if you switch it on.

If you scan an API key's QR code, Sleevesight asks for the camera then, and only then. It reads the code from the camera's frames on the phone; no frame is kept or sent. A code of several keys can be made on a computer at `sleevesight.app/api-keys`: the page draws it in the browser, makes no network request and stores nothing, so the keys do not leave the computer except in the code you scan.

If you keep your API keys in a password manager, it can fill a key's field from your saved entries, as it fills a password in any app. Sleevesight never asks it to save a key: when the key's dialog goes, it ends the autofill request unsaved, so the password manager does not offer to keep the key as a new login. One case is Android's, not Sleevesight's: if you ask for a fill by hand (*Autofill* in the field's menu) while the field already holds a key, Android sends what the field holds to the password manager with that request, as it does in any app.

If you type into a field from a computer (*Type on a computer*), Sleevesight asks for Bluetooth (*Nearby devices*) then, and only then. The page on the computer, `sleevesight.app/keyboard.html`, sends the text to the phone over Bluetooth, encrypted with a key it makes for that visit and shows only in its QR code; the page makes no network request, and nothing goes through a server. The phone forgets the key when you close the dialog; it keeps only the session's random identifier, so the same code cannot be used twice. While the dialog's session runs, the phone advertises over Bluetooth a service named for that identifier, which any device nearby that scans for Bluetooth can see; it does not advertise the phone's name, so the computer lists it as *Unknown or Unsupported Device*. With *Share phone's name with Type on a computer* on (under *Secrets*, off by default), it advertises the phone's Bluetooth name too, during the session only, and anyone nearby scanning for Bluetooth can see that name meanwhile. Either way, a device that connects to the phone can read its name, as Bluetooth lets any connected device do.

## What leaves the phone, and to whom

Requests go straight from your phone to each service. None passes through a server of Sleevesight's.

- **To the sources you switch on** (MusicBrainz, Wikipedia and Wikidata, Last.fm, and the Exa web search): the title and artists of the playing track, cleaned of tags such as "2006 Remaster"; Exa also gets the film or show a title says it is from. Wikidata gets only identifiers (from MusicBrainz or Wikipedia), never a name. For the genre labels, Last.fm also gets the album name, the only source that does: its tags for the track and for the album are asked for by the first artist's name with the title, and with the album name. While *Proactive* is on, as it is by default, the title and artists of the next track in the player's queue also go to MusicBrainz, if switched on, before that track plays.
- **To the AI service you choose** (Anthropic, OpenAI or Google): the title, artists and album, and what the sources found. This is for the playing track only. Each insight is a request of its own, with no conversation kept between them. It is made with your API key, so the service handles it under its terms for your account. The app also asks that service for its list of models, with your key and nothing about your listening: when it starts, to check that the selected model is still offered, and when you open the model choice.
- **Your API keys** go only to the service each belongs to, with each request to it; a fill you ask for by hand also passes the field's key to your password manager, on the phone ([above](#what-the-app-reads-on-the-phone)).
- **To Spotify**, only if you connect a Spotify account, once the app offers that: requests for the playlist or album being played, made with your own token.
- **To the server a cover's web address names**, when a player gives the cover only as a web address: a request for that image. The address itself may identify the album or episode.
- **To the developer, only if you report a problem with an insight**: *Report a problem*, on each insight, asks what is wrong (*Offensive or inappropriate*, *Wrong* or *Other*), takes an optional note, and shows the whole report before the app hands it on. *Open in mail app* then hands the report to your own mail app, addressed to [support@sleevesight.app](mailto:support@sleevesight.app), and it reaches the developer only if you send it from there. Until you send it, your mail app handles it as any message you write: some keep a draft in your mail account, with your mail provider. It holds the reason and your note; the track as the player reported it (title, artists, album and length) and the MusicBrainz identifiers found for it; the insight, and the evidence lines it was written from (for an insight kept from before, every evidence line of the track, since the app keeps no record of which ones it was written from), with their sources and web addresses; and the prompt version, AI service, model and reasoning effort it was written with. Never your API keys, the history or the settings, and nothing about the player or the phone. Your mail app sends it from your own email address, as any email you write, and the developer receives it as such ([below](#what-reaches-the-developer)). *Copy report*, offered beside *Open in mail app*, copies the whole report instead, to send however you like, with no mail app on the phone too.
- **To the developer, only if you send a tester's report**: in developer mode, with its *Report button* setting at *Show* or *Auto* (developer mode is on, and *Report button* at *Auto*, by default in the test build that Google Play's testing tracks get; developer mode is off, and *Report button* at *Hide*, in any other), a small button on the home screen collects what the app knows at that moment into a report, and shows the whole report before the app hands it on. It holds when you tapped, with your time zone, and the app's version and build; your settings, never your API keys; what the player reports (which player, the title, artists, album, length and the player's identifier for the track, whether it is playing and how far in); what the app made of it: what each source found, the evidence lines with their web addresses, the insight with its citation check, and the AI service, model and reasoning effort it was written with; and the app's diagnostics: the latest events of its current run, and what skip detection has learned. The track changes recorded on the phone (the latest 100, with the tracks' titles and times) are in it only if you turn them on in the report. Each part can be edited or left out, and you can add a note. It holds only what the app itself holds: its settings, what it received from the player, the sources and the AI service, and its diagnostics; never your API keys, and nothing else about your phone. As with a problem report, *Open in mail app* hands it to your own mail app, addressed to [support@sleevesight.app](mailto:support@sleevesight.app), it reaches the developer only if you send it from there, your mail app handles it until then as any message you write, and with no mail app it can be copied instead.
- **Over Bluetooth, to devices nearby**, only while a key's dialog is typing on a computer (*Type on a computer*): the phone advertises a service named for the session's random identifier, which any device nearby that scans for Bluetooth can see, and, only with *Share phone's name with Type on a computer* on, the phone's Bluetooth name. What you type travels from the computer to the phone, encrypted with the session's key; the phone sends nothing back but Bluetooth's acknowledgements.
- **To a file you choose**, only if you export the diagnostics: with the *Diagnostics* developer setting on, *Export* writes what the diagnostics screen shows (the latest events of the app's current run, the track changes recorded, what skip detection has learned, and the caches' sizes) to a file where you choose, which may be a cloud folder. That copy is yours, outside the app.

Every request is made over HTTPS; Android refuses the app any unencrypted connection, so a cover given as an `http://` address is not fetched.

Each of these services also sees your phone's IP address, as every service you connect to does. What each does with what it receives is set by its own policy: [MetaBrainz](https://metabrainz.org/privacy) (MusicBrainz), the [Wikimedia Foundation](https://foundation.wikimedia.org/wiki/Policy:Privacy_policy) (Wikipedia and Wikidata), [Last.fm](https://www.last.fm/legal/privacy), [Exa](https://exa.ai/privacy-policy), [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/privacy), [OpenAI](https://openai.com/policies/privacy-policy/), [Google](https://policies.google.com/privacy) (with the [Gemini API terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms)).

By default, lookups and writing happen only while Sleevesight is on screen. With *Look up in the background* on, they also happen while it is not.

Nothing else leaves the phone.

## What stays on the phone

- The length of each track. Its cover is held only in memory, for the current track, and never stored.
- The history: the tracks Sleevesight has seen play, the record of track changes it uses to tell your skips from natural ends, and what it has learned from them for each player.
- What the sources found for each track, kept for a while so the same track is not looked up again (the lifetimes are in the settings).
- Only if you switch on *Keep what sources said about each artist* (off by default): what the sources said about each artist played (Last.fm's answers and tags, the Wikipedia artist article and its Wikidata facts, MusicBrainz's tags), and Last.fm's tags for each album played, kept per artist (and per album) and with each track's findings, for the same lifetimes, so another track by the same artist does not look the artist up again. Kept together, it amounts to a record of the artists you have played, which is why it is off unless you choose it. Off, none of it is kept (nor the sources' verdicts on artists' pages, nor the artists' MusicBrainz ids), and each track's artists are looked up again, so their names go to the sources you switch on with every track; of a track's findings, only the web search's findings (pages about the recording, which may name or describe its artists) and those of a lookup that found no artist to look up are kept, so a track played before is looked up again at MusicBrainz (its match held in memory for half an hour only), Wikipedia and Last.fm, unless it has no credited artist, and lookups are slower and more requests go out. Off keeps less on the phone and sends more out; on sends fewer requests about artists and keeps more. Either way, each track's own lookups send its artists' names, the history and the kept findings record which tracks you played, and the insights written may retell some of what the sources said. Turning it off deletes what was kept, and so did the first start of the version that added the switch, for what earlier versions kept without asking. MusicBrainz's answers, whatever the switch, are held in the app's memory for half an hour, for the track about to play, and never written to the phone's storage.
- The insights written, your settings, and your API keys. The keys are encrypted with a key kept in the phone's Android Keystore, are never shown again once saved, and are never logged.

None of this is part of Android's backup or of a transfer to a new phone.

## What reaches the developer

Nothing the app sends on its own: the developer of Sleevesight runs no server, so nothing about you or your listening reaches them unless you send it. What does reach them is only email you send: a report of a problem with an insight or a tester's report, each of which you see in full and send from your own mail app, or any other email you write to [support@sleevesight.app](mailto:support@sleevesight.app). The developer reads a problem report to find out what made the insight wrong or offensive, and to change what the app asks of the AI services or what it checks, so the same does not happen again; and a tester's report to find and fix what went wrong in the app. Email to the developer, a report included, with your email address, is kept by the email service that handles the address, which processes it for the developer, for as long as the conversation or the fix needs, and deleted when you ask, unless the law requires keeping it. Beyond that service, none of it is sold or shared.

Google Play, which distributes the app, handles your installation under [Google's privacy policy](https://policies.google.com/privacy). It gives the developer aggregated install statistics and, from phones whose owners allow usage and diagnostics sharing, crash reports that name no one.

## Your choices

- Each source is a switch, and *Show insights* off makes no AI calls at all. *Show genres* off asks no source for genres, and leaves Last.fm's tags and Wikidata's genres out of what goes to the AI service.
- *Reactive* sets whether an insight is written for every track, only when you skip, or only when you tap Write.
- *Proactive* off sends nothing about the next track.
- *Report a problem*, and the tester's report button, send nothing by themselves: each shows the report and hands it to your mail app, which handles it as any message you write (some keep a draft in your mail account) and sends it to the developer only if you do. A tester's report leaves out the track changes unless you turn them on, and any part you leave out. *Report button* at *Hide*, or developer mode off, removes the button.
- *Keep what sources said about each artist*, off by default, keeps what the sources said about each artist played on the phone, so fewer requests about artists are sent; turning it off deletes it.
- *Keep history for* sets how long the history is kept, from a week to a year, or forever. *Clear history* deletes it at once.
- A saved key can be removed in the settings, with the delete button on its row, and *Purge keys* removes them all.
- Android's *Clear storage* for Sleevesight, or uninstalling it, deletes everything it kept on the phone. Cached findings also expire on their own, on the lifetimes shown in the settings.

What a service keeps of your requests, such as Anthropic for your account, is between you and that service.

## The GDPR

The EU's General Data Protection Regulation ([Regulation (EU) 2016/679](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj), the GDPR) covers personal data, which here is what the app sends about what you play, with your phone's IP address, and any email you send. This section and the next say what the app does and how its developer reads its position under these laws. They are not legal advice.

### Who is responsible for what

- **Email to the developer.** For email you send to [support@sleevesight.app](mailto:support@sleevesight.app), a report from the app included, the developer, Waclaw Kusnierczyk, is the controller, the one who decides what is done with it (Article 4(7)).
- **This website.** GitHub serves it, and logs each visitor's IP address for security, under [its own privacy statement](https://docs.github.com/en/pages/getting-started-with-github-pages/about-github-pages#data-collection). The design and documentation pages with diagrams also have your browser fetch the diagram script from jsDelivr, run by Volentio JSD Limited and served by its CDN providers, which see your IP address too. The developer receives no logs and no analytics from either. Under Directive 95/46, which the GDPR replaced, the Court of Justice of the EU held that a website's operator whose page has its visitors' browsers send their data to a third party "can be considered to be a controller" of that collection and disclosure by transmission ([*Fashion ID*, C‑40/17](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62017CJ0040), point 2 of the ruling), jointly with the third party (paragraphs 76 and 84). So the developer takes that role for this site: for your IP address reaching GitHub and jsDelivr when you visit, not for what each does with it afterwards, which is theirs.
- **The app.** Nothing the app sends on its own reaches the developer; a report is email you send (above). Each request goes from your phone straight to the service it is for, and that service decides what it does with what it receives, under its own terms and policy (linked [above](#what-leaves-the-phone-and-to-whom)). The AI services handle your requests under your own account: Anthropic's privacy policy, for one, says it does not cover what Anthropic processes on behalf of its API customers, which its [commercial terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms) and data processing addendum cover instead.
- **The open question.** The developer designs the app, so the developer decides what a request carries and to which services it can go, though it never receives one. The Court of Justice of the EU has held that someone who influences the processing of personal data for their own purposes can be a controller without ever having access to the data ([*Jehovan todistajat*, C‑25/17](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62017CJ0025), paragraphs 68 and 69; [C‑683/21](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62021CJ0683), paragraphs 30 and 31), and that an entity that had a mobile app developed can be a controller of what the app processes (C‑683/21, point 1 of the ruling). The developer's reading is that it is not a controller of what the app sends: it has no purpose of its own in those requests, which are made for you, to the services you switch on, with your own keys, and it gets nothing from them. Your own use of the app, to read about the music you play, is in the developer's reading a purely personal activity, which the GDPR does not cover (Article 2(2)(c)). Recital 18 adds that the GDPR "applies to controllers or processors which provide the means for processing personal data for such personal or household activities": it binds the services that receive your requests, and would bind the developer only if the developer were a controller or processor of them, which is the question above. Whatever the answer, the app is designed to send as little as it can: each service gets only what it needs (the album only to the AI service, only the artists to Last.fm, only identifiers to Wikidata), each flow can be switched off, and this policy says what goes where.

### On what basis

- **Email:** the developer's legitimate interest in answering you, and, for a report from the app, in fixing what made the insight wrong or offensive, or what went wrong in the app (Article 6(1)(f)). Email to the developer, a report included, with your email address, is kept by the email service that handles the address, which processes it for the developer, for as long as the conversation or the fix needs, and deleted when you ask, unless the law requires keeping it.
- **This website:** the developer's legitimate interest in publishing the app's documentation and this policy (Article 6(1)(f)): the site is served by GitHub Pages, and the design and documentation pages draw their diagrams with jsDelivr's script.
- **The app:** none is needed by the developer, which processes nothing of what the app sends. Each service you use states its own basis for what it receives.

### Your rights

The GDPR gives you the right to see your data, have it corrected or erased, restrict or object to its processing, and take a copy with you (Articles 15 to 21). For what the app keeps, all of it on your phone, they are in your hands:

- **To see it and take a copy:** only part of it can be seen or copied in the app today. The home screen shows the playing track, what the sources found for it, and its insight. With the *Diagnostics* developer setting on, the diagnostics screen shows the latest events of the app's current run (a few hundred at most, held only in memory and gone when the app's process ends), the track changes recorded (kept with the history), what skip detection has learned, and the caches' sizes, and *Export* writes those to a file you choose. The export is a diagnostic record, not a copy of everything the app keeps: it leaves out the list of tracks played, the insights written, the cached findings and the settings, and no screen lists the tracks played or the insights of earlier tracks. An export of the tracks played with their insights is planned for a later version.
- **To erase it:** *Clear history* deletes the list of tracks played and the record of track changes at once. It does not delete the insights written or the cached findings: the insights stay until newer ones push them out of the cache's size limit, and the findings until they expire, on the lifetimes shown in the settings. Turning *Keep what sources said about each artist* off deletes what the sources said about artists, per artist and in the findings. Each saved key has a delete button, and *Purge keys* removes them all. Android's *Clear storage* for Sleevesight, or uninstalling it, deletes everything it kept, the insights included.
- **To object, or restrict what is sent:** each source is a switch, *Show insights* off makes no AI calls, *Proactive* off sends nothing about the next track, and *Look up in the background*, off by default, keeps every request to the times the app is on screen ([Your choices](#your-choices)).
- **For what a service keeps** of your requests, ask that service: its privacy policy, linked above, says how.
- **For what the developer holds,** which is only email you sent, reports from the app included, write to [support@sleevesight.app](mailto:support@sleevesight.app) for a copy or its deletion. You get an answer within one month (Article 12(3)).

## The EU AI Act

The EU's AI Act ([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj), as amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI, [Regulation (EU) 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj)) sets duties according to the role each party has.

### Where Sleevesight stands

- **What it is:** an AI system (Article 3(1)) that writes its insights with a general-purpose AI model (Article 3(63)) from the AI service you choose: Claude from Anthropic, GPT from OpenAI, or Gemini from Google. The developer is its provider (Article 3(3)), a *downstream provider* of the model it uses (Article 3(68)); each AI service is the provider of its own model, with the duties the Act sets for such models.
- **Not high-risk, and nothing prohibited:** writing a few sentences about a song is none of the uses the Act prohibits (Article 5) or lists as high-risk (Article 6 and Annex III).
- **You are not a deployer:** a deployer, in the Act, is someone using an AI system under their authority "except where the AI system is used in the course of a personal non-professional activity" (Article 3(4)), and the Act does not apply to the duties of people using one that way (Article 2(10)). Reading about the music you play is such use.
- **Free software is not exempt from transparency:** the Act leaves out AI systems released under free and open-source licences, as Sleevesight is (GPL 3), unless they are prohibited, high-risk, or covered by the transparency duties of Article 50 (Article 2(12)). Sleevesight is covered by Article 50, so those duties apply to it.

### Transparency (Article 50)

**What the app tells you.** Before anything is written, the first-run screen says that the AI service you choose writes the insights, and nothing is written until you choose one, and save its key, in the settings. Each insight is written by the service and model set there at the time; the card does not yet name them. Each sentence of an insight shows the evidence it cites, from the sources the app looked up; a sentence that cites nothing is underlined; and a sentence from the model's own knowledge, which is used only if you switch it on, carries an *M* chip in place of a source's, which a screen reader reads as "From the model's own knowledge, not verified", and the line under the insight counts such sentences.

**Interacting with an AI (Article 50(1)).** This duty is for AI systems meant to interact directly with people: they must be designed so that people know they are dealing with an AI system, unless that is obvious to a reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect person. The developer's reading, which is arguable, is that the insights are outside it: they are written for you, and you do not exchange messages with the model. But the Commission's [guidelines on Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/guidelines-transparency-obligations-providers-and-deployers-ai-systems) (C(2026) 5054, 20 July 2026, point 30(ii)) allow even a single prompt and its reply to be an interaction, where the exchange has a conversational or responsive character, including one the person did not start, so another reading puts a tap on *Write insight* inside it. Either way, the app gives the information the duty asks for, and when Article 50(5) asks for it, at the first interaction at the latest: its first-run screen opens by saying that it "has the AI service you choose write about" what is playing, before any insight is written, and you choose that service, and save its key, yourself. Follow-up questions to the AI, planned for a later version, will be an exchange beyond doubt; when they come, the screen they are asked on will say that the answers come from an AI model.

**Machine-readable marking (Article 50(2)).** Article 50(2) requires providers of AI systems that generate text to ensure that the outputs are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated, and that their technical solutions are effective, interoperable, robust and reliable as far as this is technically feasible, taking into account the kind of content, the costs of implementation and the generally acknowledged state of the art. It does not apply to the extent a system performs an assistive function for standard editing or does not substantially alter the input it is given; the insights are new text written by the model. It applies from 2 August 2026; systems already on the market before that date have until 2 December 2026 (the Act's Article 111(4), added by the Omnibus), and Sleevesight was not on the market before 2 August 2026: its first build went to testers in October 2026. The Commission's [Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content) (June 2026), which the developer has not signed, describes the techniques (Measure 1.1): digitally signed metadata, where the content's format can carry it, which it finds free-form text cannot; an imperceptible watermark, added as the model generates the text or afterwards ("post-hoc"), except on "very short text", under 200 tokens; and marking done elsewhere in the value chain, by an upstream model provider or a third party, which a system's provider may rely on "without prejudice to" its own responsibility that its system's outputs are marked. What the app does: its first-run screen says that the AI service you choose writes the insights; each sentence shows what it cites, and a sentence from the model's own knowledge carries an *M* chip; it adds no machine-readable mark of its own, neither as the text is written nor afterwards; and it asks the model for two to four sentences, without enforcing that length. Whether Anthropic, OpenAI or Google mark their output, the developer does not know. The insights are shown only on the app's own screen, kept only on the phone, and in no export. Once insights can leave the app, in the export of the tracks played with their insights planned for a later version, the exported file will mark each one as AI-written, with the service and model that wrote it. The developer is looking at how to meet the duty.

**Text published on matters of public interest (Article 50(4)).** This is a duty of deployers who publish such text, which is not how the app is used.

## Children, and the AI services' own terms

Sleevesight is not directed at children under 13, and sets no minimum age of its own beyond that. It works without any AI service, and switches none on by default. An AI service is used on your own key, under your own agreement with its provider, and using it means agreeing to that provider's terms, which may set a minimum age or limit the use they allow. Each service's key dialog in the settings says what its terms require, and links to them. As checked on 24 October 2026:

- **Anthropic:** its [Commercial Terms of Service](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms) (effective 17 June 2025) cover use of its API keys. They set no minimum age, and say that "Services under these Terms are not for consumer use"; Anthropic's [help centre](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/8987200) adds that "individuals and hobbyists are welcome to use the Claude API", under those terms.
- **OpenAI:** its [Services Agreement](https://openai.com/policies/services-agreement/) (effective 1 January 2026) applies to its APIs "for customers who are businesses and developers", and its customer agrees not to "allow minors to use OpenAI Services without consent from their parent or guardian". It sets no age of its own.
- **Google:** its [Gemini API terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms) (effective 23 March 2026) say: "You must be 18 years of age or older to use the APIs", and that the Gemini API "is for developers building with Google AI models for professional or business purposes, not for consumer use".

Terms change; the provider's page is the one that counts.
